How it works

The part that makes money is the part nobody watches.

A traveller books once and moves on. For the next several weeks a loop asks the airline the same question over and over, and when the answer changes it swaps the ticket underneath them. Same flight. Same seat. Same departure time. Only the money moves.

Book the flight here instead of with the airline

This is the only step a person performs, and it exists for one structural reason: whoever issues the ticket is the only party who can later exchange it. Booking through the platform is what makes everything after this possible.

Search and select

Route, dates, cabin. Results show carrier, flight number, times and price — the information someone booking a work trip actually reads.

Say what can't be watched

Non-changeable fares can never be exchanged. Flag that at the point of choosing, not after. A traveller who picks one should know it won't be monitored.

Passenger and payment

Prefilled from the company's traveller profile. Card details go through hosted fields so they never touch our servers.

Confirm, and start watching

The order is created and we become the ticket's owner of record. The confirmation screen is where monitoring gets explained, because it's the only screen guaranteed to be read.

Poll, match, price, repeat

The loop runs on its own for weeks. How often it runs depends on how close the flight is — fares two months out barely move, fares inside a week move constantly, and polling both at the same rate is how the search costs eat the margin.

Poll on a sliding schedule

Daily beyond 30 days out. Every six hours inside 30. Hourly inside 7. Then stop entirely 24 hours before departure, when an exchange would be more disruption than it's worth.

Match the exact flight

Same carrier, same flight numbers, same order. A cheaper seat that reroutes someone through a connection isn't a saving, it's a complaint.

Search once for many trips

Fifty bookings on one route and date collapse into a single lookup. The cache is for deciding what's worth pricing — it can't be reused to actually book.

Let the airline do the maths

Comparing two ticket prices ignores fare rules and gets the answer wrong. Ask the airline what the exchange costs and use change_total_amount. It already nets out the penalty.

Every poll runs the same checks in the same order. Most polls stop at the first or second gate and go back to waiting. Occasionally one clears all five.

One operation, not two

The exchange is a single call. Cancelling and rebooking separately opens a window where someone is double-booked or holding nothing at all.

Tell them, briefly

New ticket, same flight. The notification exists so nobody panics at a new booking reference — not because anything about their day changed.

Show the work, then invoice

The travel manager never watches the loop. They see the result once a month, and the result has to be legible enough to defend to whoever signs off on the spend.

Per trip, what changed

What was paid, what it became, what came back. A price history for the monitoring window makes the number checkable rather than asserted.

Per month, what it cost

Total recovered, our share, net to the company. One line per exchange, traceable to a booking reference.

Unresolved — affects the whole model

When an exchange returns money, it may come back as cash to the original card or as an airline credit locked to that carrier and that passenger's name. A credit is worth far less than cash, and a percentage fee charged against one is a much harder conversation.

The sandbox returns a fixed value for every exchange, so this can't be settled before live access. Until it is, the settlement design should assume either outcome is possible.

Flights only. Same-carrier exchanges. Behaviour shown here reflects the documented API and the sandbox spike — the economics remain unvalidated against live fares.
